Taking the slow train north

The overnight train leaves at ten, and by the time the city lights thin out the dining car is already half empty. Nobody here is in a hurry, which is the whole point.

Flying would have taken ninety minutes. The train takes eleven hours, two changes and one long wait on a platform where the only open kiosk sells coffee and crossword books.

Somewhere past midnight the landscape turns to forest. You stop checking the time and start noticing things instead: a lit farmhouse, a level crossing, a fox that does not bother to run.

By morning the air through the window is colder and smells of pine. The conductor knows most passengers by name, and the last stop is a town with one street and a very good bakery.

Slow travel is not about the distance. It is about letting the journey become part of the trip, instead of the hours you try to skip.
